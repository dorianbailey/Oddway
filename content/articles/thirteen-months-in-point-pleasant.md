On the night of 15 November 1966, two young couples — Roger and Linda
Scarberry, Steve and Mary Mallette — drove out to the abandoned munitions plant
north of Point Pleasant, West Virginia. Locals called it the TNT Area: rows of
concrete igloos built to store explosives during the war, gone to weeds.

They said something was standing near the old power house. Around seven feet
tall. Grey. Wings folded behind its back. Eyes that threw the headlights back
red.

They drove for town at a hundred miles an hour and told the Mason County
sheriff about it in the early hours of the morning. That statement is the first
official record of the thing.

## The name came from a newspaper desk

Not from the witnesses. Somebody in a newsroom, with the Batman television
series at the height of its popularity and a villain called Killer Moth already
in circulation, filed it as **Mothman**, and the wires carried the word out of
West Virginia.

It was not even the first report. Three days earlier, five men digging a grave
near Clendenin had watched something human-shaped pass overhead.

Over the following thirteen months more than a hundred encounters were reported
across Mason County. Police took statements. The local paper printed names. The
descriptions stayed oddly consistent: tall, grey, silent, red-eyed.

Officials suggested a sandhill crane, or a great blue heron, or a barred owl —
a large bird outside its usual range, seen badly at night. That explanation
covers most of the reports, and almost nobody in town accepted it.

## 15 December 1967

At rush hour, the Silver Bridge over the Ohio River — the crossing between
Point Pleasant and Gallipolis — failed in seconds. Forty-six people died.

The investigation was clear. A single eyebar in the suspension chain had a
crack roughly a tenth of an inch deep, grown by stress-corrosion over decades,
invisible to the inspections of the day. The design had no redundancy: when
that one link went, the whole structure followed. It changed how the country
inspects bridges.

There is no official finding of any kind connecting the sightings to the
collapse. There is no mechanism by which there could be.

What there is, is the date. The reports stopped that afternoon.

## What the story is actually about

It is worth being careful here, because forty-six people died and the town has
had to live alongside what grew out of it.

John Keel, who investigated in person and published *The Mothman Prophecies* in
the mid-1970s, framed the thirteen months as omen. The 2002 film sharpened that
into something close to prophecy. Neither is what the witnesses described at
the time — they described being frightened by something in a field.

Linda Scarberry, years later: *I wish we had never seen it.*

Point Pleasant now has a museum, a metal statue on Fourth Street, and a
festival every September that fills the town. The commodification of a
disaster's shadow is a genuinely uncomfortable thing, and the better parts of
Point Pleasant know it. The bridge memorial and the Mothman statue are a
four-minute walk apart, and visiting one without the other misses the story.

## Going there

The TNT Area is now the McClintic Wildlife Management Area, open to the public
and criss-crossed with trails. The igloos are still there. It became a
Superfund site in 1983 and parts remain under cleanup, so read the signage and
stay on the trails — that is the ordinary hazard, and it is a real one.
