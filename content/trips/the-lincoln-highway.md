The Lincoln Highway was the first road built to cross America, opened in 1913,
and the Pennsylvania stretch became the template for everything that followed:
a two-lane road, small towns strung along it, and businesses that had to make
you stop.

Most of what survives here exists because of that road. The turnpike opened in
1940 and took the traffic; the buildings stayed.

## What that produced

**The Bedford Coffee Pot** is the clearest example. Bert Koontz built an
eighteen-foot coffee pot in 1927 to pull motorists into the service station next
door. It is an advertisement in the shape of the thing being advertised, and
there is a whole vocabulary of these buildings along the old road.

**The Haines Shoe House** at Hellam is the other, built in 1948 by a shoe
magnate as a guesthouse and a billboard at once. It is a short-term rental now,
so photograph it from the roadside, which the owners explicitly invite.

**The Giant Quarter** at Everett and **Mister Ed's Elephant Museum** — twelve
thousand elephants and a candy counter — are the same instinct in later decades.

## The darker middle

**Kecksburg** starts the route: the acorn behind the fire station, built as a
prop for an Unsolved Mysteries episode in 1990 and kept.

**The Seven Gates of Hell** on Trout Run Road is a legend about an asylum that
never existed, attached to land that is private. Stay on the public road.

**The Albatwitch** at Chickies Rock is Lancaster County's apple-stealing
cryptid, four feet tall and reportedly a poor sport about being watched.

## Ending in Philadelphia

**Eastern State** and the **Mütter** in one day is a lot, and worth it. One is
the prison that invented solitary confinement; the other is a nineteenth-century
medical teaching collection that has never pretended to be anything else.

## Practical

Three days at a genuine Lincoln Highway pace. US-30 is slower than the turnpike
by design — that is the entire point, and rushing it defeats the exercise.

The Gettysburg stretch in summer is busy. Go early or go midweek.
