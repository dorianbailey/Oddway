Everything else in this index is provisional.

A museum built by one person closes when that person dies. A fibreglass giant
gets sold to a collector in another state. A themed motel is repainted, a cave
tour loses its insurance, a roadside attraction is bulldozed for a slip road.
Work on an index like this for long enough and you develop a low-grade anxiety
about whether anything you listed last year is still standing.

Then there are the graves. Sixty-five of them, in twenty-one states. Every one
will be exactly where it is now in fifty years, because in almost every American
jurisdiction moving a burial requires a court order, next-of-kin consent, or
both.

## What ends up in one

**Dr John Pemberton** is in Linwood Cemetery in Columbus, Georgia. He invented
Coca-Cola, sold the rights for a sum variously reported as somewhere around two
thousand dollars, and died in poverty two years later. The company he started is
one of the largest on earth. The grave is a modest stone in a municipal
cemetery, and the city's public works department is the authority you contact
about it.

**Chang and Eng Bunker** are buried at White Plains Baptist Church in Mount
Airy, North Carolina. They were the conjoined twins from whom the phrase
"Siamese twins" came, they toured for decades, then bought farmland, married two
sisters, and between them had twenty-one children. They are in a single grave
because they died within hours of one another.

**Charles Stratton** — General Tom Thumb — has a monument in Bridgeport,
Connecticut that is considerably larger than he was, which was the point, and
which his employer P. T. Barnum would have understood perfectly.

## And then the ones that make no sense at all

In Middlebury, Vermont there is the grave of **Amum-Her-Khepesh-Ef**, an
Egyptian child who died around 1883 BC. He arrived in the nineteenth century as
part of a museum acquisition, was eventually found in poor condition, and was
given a headstone and a burial in a Vermont cemetery by people who decided that
was the decent thing to do.

In Suttons Bay, Michigan there is an **Archduke of Austria**. In Lambertville,
New Jersey there is **George Coryell**, whose entire claim is that he was one of
the men who lowered George Washington's coffin — a fact recorded on a marker
because it is the only thing anybody knows about him.

And in Chardon, Ohio, **Ettore Boiardi** is buried under the name the label
spelled phonetically so Americans could pronounce it.

## Why they accumulate

A grave becomes a destination through a particular sequence. Somebody is briefly
extremely famous. The fame decays. The buildings associated with them are sold
or demolished, the objects go into private collections, the business changes
hands and drops the name.

What survives is the one object nobody has the authority to remove. So the
grave, which was never intended to be a monument to the interesting fact, ends
up being the only place the interesting fact is still attached to.

This is not a morbid kind of tourism. It is closer to the opposite — it is the
last physical index of a person, in a country that is otherwise very willing to
knock things down.

## If you go

They are almost all in working cemeteries with other people's recent grief in
them. Go quietly, in daylight, and do not move anything that has been left.

Rubbings damage stone. A photograph does not.
