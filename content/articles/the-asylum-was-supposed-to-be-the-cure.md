A quarter of a mile of hand-cut sandstone stands on a hillside in Weston, West
Virginia, with wings stepping back from a central block in a long shallow V.

It looks like that for a reason, and the reason is a theory of medicine.

## The Kirkbride idea

Thomas Story Kirkbride was a Philadelphia physician who argued in the
mid-nineteenth century that the environment was itself the treatment. Not
restraint. Not the chains and cages of the madhouses he was reacting against.
**Light, air, quiet, space and a view.**

He specified the buildings in detail. Long wings, stepped back so every ward got
sunlight from more than one side and cross-ventilation through the whole depth.
Wards graded so patients moved between them as they improved. Farmland attached,
so there was work to do outdoors. A generous limit on population — Kirkbride
said 250 patients, and was firm about it.

Dozens went up across America. Trans-Allegheny is one of the largest, begun in
1858, interrupted by the Civil War, and taking patients from 1864.

The men who built it thought they were building a cure.

## What happened instead

The same thing that happened to Eastern State, in the same century, for the same
reason.

Capacity was the whole design, and capacity was the first thing abandoned. A
building specified for 250 was holding something in the order of ten times that
by the middle of the twentieth century. Wards designed for airflow and quiet
were filled with beds until neither was possible.

And the population changed. Asylums like this became the place where states put
people they had nowhere else to put: the elderly, the epileptic, the alcoholic,
people with head injuries, people whose families could not manage them. Much of
that had nothing to do with what Kirkbride designed for.

Overcrowding on that scale produces the things the ghost tours describe. It does
not require anybody to be a villain, which is the uncomfortable part. The
building did not fail because cruel people ran it. It failed because a humane
idea was funded once, filled past its limit, and then left running for a
century.

It closed in 1994.

## Why it matters that the building survived

Most Kirkbrides were demolished. They were enormous, expensive to heat, and
associated with a chapter nobody wanted a monument to.

This one is still here because somebody bought it at auction and has been
holding the roof on ever since, partly by running tours. The paranormal
programmes fund the daytime ones, and the daytime ones are about architecture
and medicine — the ventilation, the ward system, what the therapeutic theory
actually claimed.

You can walk a corridor designed by a man who believed a window in the right
place could return someone to themselves. That is worth more than a story about
footsteps.

## Going there

Daytime history tours and after-dark paranormal ones run separately and are
priced separately. Take the history tour first, even if the ghost tour is what
brought you — the second one lands very differently once you know what the
rooms were for.

It is a large, cold, largely unheated stone building. Dress for that.
