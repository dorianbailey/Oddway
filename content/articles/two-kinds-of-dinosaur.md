There are fifty-seven dinosaurs in this index, spread across twenty-eight
states, and they fall into two piles that have almost nothing to do with each
other.

## The ones that are actually there

**Dinosaur Ridge** outside Morrison, Colorado is a road cutting through a
hogback where a highway crew in 1937 exposed a bedding plane covered in
footprints. You walk along a pavement and the tracks are in the rock beside
your head, at eye level, because the whole formation has been tipped on end.

**Dinosaur State Park** in Rocky Hill, Connecticut exists because a bulldozer
operator clearing ground for a state building in 1966 turned over a slab, saw
what was on it, and stopped. There are around two thousand tracks under the
dome. The building project was cancelled.

**Holyoke**, Massachusetts has footprints in a riverside ledge that were
recognised in the 1800s — a period when the leading local theory held that they
had been made by enormous birds, which is closer to right than it sounded at the
time.

These places share a quality: nobody chose the location. The animal chose it, a
hundred and fifty million years before there was a state to put a park in.

## The ones somebody built

Then there is **Cabazon**, where a restaurant owner spent years building a
concrete Brontosaurus beside Interstate 10 so that traffic would stop for
lunch. It took over a decade. A Tyrannosaurus followed. The restaurant is gone
and the dinosaurs are still there, which is the usual ratio.

**Dinosaur Land** in White Post, Virginia. **Dinosaur Gardens** in Ossineke,
Michigan, begun in the 1930s by one man over about twenty years. **Galleta
Meadows** in Borrego Springs, California, where a landowner commissioned metal
sculptures and scattered them across open desert — not only dinosaurs, but also
a sloth, a mammoth, and a serpent several hundred feet long.

And **Dinosaur Park** in Rapid City, South Dakota, built by the Works Progress
Administration in 1936. Five concrete animals on a hill above the town, put up
as public works during the Depression, and now old enough to be documented by
the Library of Congress as architecture.

## The part that connects to the giants

Some of the fibreglass ones have a family resemblance to something else on this
site, and it is not a coincidence.

International Fiberglass of Venice, California — the company that turned one
Paul Bunyan mould into hundreds of cowboys, Vikings and muffler-holding
lumberjacks — also made dinosaurs. Sinclair Oil used a Brontosaurus as its
trademark, and needed one outside a great many petrol stations. The same
workshop, the same material, the same period, the same reason: something large
enough to be seen from a car at speed.

So a roadside dinosaur from the 1960s and a Muffler Man from the 1960s are
often siblings. They were sold by the same salesman, out of the same catalogue,
to people with the same problem.

## Which pile is worth the detour

Both, for different reasons, and it is worth knowing which you are going to
before you arrive.

A trackway is a genuinely strange experience: an animal walked across wet mud
one afternoon and the mud is still there, still holding the shape of the
afternoon. Nothing about it is designed for you.

A concrete Brontosaurus is the opposite — entirely designed for you, by
somebody who needed you to pull over. That is its own kind of honest.

The mistake is arriving at one expecting the other.
