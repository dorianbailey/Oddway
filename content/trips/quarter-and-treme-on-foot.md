Start outside the Quarter, in Tremé, because that is where the order of things
actually runs.

Under the French and Spanish, enslaved people in New Orleans had Sundays. They
gathered in the open ground north of the ramparts to trade, to dance, and to
play drums — which was banned almost everywhere else in the American South. The
rhythms that survived there are traceable through brass bands and jazz to most
of what American popular music became.

That ground is **Congo Square**, and it is three streets from the bars on
Bourbon that could not exist without it.

Two miles, flat, the whole thing on cracked pavement in heat.

## Tremé

Outside St. Augustine Church is the **Tomb of the Unknown Slave** — a marine
chain hung with shackles, set upright in a small garden, for the unnamed people
buried in the surrounding ground without record. It is not large and it does
not need to be.

The **Backstreet Cultural Museum** holds Mardi Gras Indian suits, social aid
and pleasure club regalia, and jazz funeral material. The suits take a year to
sew and are worn once. Nowhere else collects this.

## St. Louis No. 1

New Orleans buries above ground, and the oldest surviving city cemetery is a
packed grid of tombs on eight acres.

Public wandering ended in 2015 after persistent vandalism, so entry is by
licensed tour or through the archdiocese. That includes the **Glapion family
tomb**, the one long identified with **Marie Laveau** — the nineteenth-century
Voodoo practitioner whose grave is among the most visited in the country, and
which people kept marking with X's in the belief it brought a wish, which is
why nobody may wander in unaccompanied any more.

## Royal Street

The **Historic Voodoo Museum** is small, cluttered and has been running since
1972, which makes it older than most of the Quarter's attractions.

The **LaLaurie Mansion** is private and stays private. In 1834 a fire at the
house exposed enslaved people held in conditions that caused a mob to gut the
building, and Delphine LaLaurie fled the city. Everything since — and there is
a great deal since, most of it invented — has been layered on top of that.
Look from the pavement and do not disturb whoever lives there.

**Lafitte's Blacksmith Shop** is an eighteenth-century Creole cottage, one of
the oldest structures in the Quarter, operating as a bar by candlelight. The
pirate connection is unproven and the building is real.

**Gallier House** is the architect's own 1857 townhouse, built with indoor
plumbing, ventilation and a cold-storage room when almost nothing had them.

## Chartres and Jackson Square

The **Pharmacy Museum** occupies America's first licensed pharmacist's shop and
displays what he sold. Leeches. Opium preparations. A fearsome case of surgical
tools. It is the single best argument on this route for having been born later.

**Muriel's** is a working restaurant that keeps a séance lounge upstairs for
Pierre Antoine Lepardi Jourdan, said to have shot himself after losing the
building at cards.

The **Presbytère** was built as a courthouse and now holds the Louisiana State
Museum's two permanent exhibits: Mardi Gras, and Hurricane Katrina. The second
is one of the more serious disaster exhibitions anywhere, and putting it beside
the costumes is a choice the museum made on purpose.

## Three bars to finish

The **Napoleon House** is named for a plan that never happened — a scheme by
the mayor to bring Bonaparte to New Orleans after St Helena, which foundered
when Bonaparte died. It has been peeling artfully ever since.

The **Old Absinthe House** has poured drinks since the 1800s and accumulated
stories about everyone from Jean Lafitte to Mark Twain.

And the **Carousel Bar** at the Monteleone genuinely revolves — the whole bar,
one slow rotation every fifteen minutes, since 1949. It is the correct place to
stop walking.

## Practical

Two miles, flat, about seventy-five minutes of walking and a full day with the
museums.

The cemetery needs booking ahead — licensed tour or archdiocese, not a
walk-up. The Backstreet Cultural Museum keeps short hours and closes Mondays.

Do this in the morning or the evening. Midday between June and September is not
a walking proposition, and the stretch from Congo Square through Tremé has
almost no shade.

Congo Square, the Tomb of the Unknown Slave and the outside of the LaLaurie
Mansion cost nothing. The last three stops cost whatever you drink.
