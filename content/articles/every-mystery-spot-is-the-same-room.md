Somewhere on a hillside near a highway there is a small wooden building leaning
noticeably to one side. Inside, a guide will stand a ball on a plank and it will
roll uphill. Water will run the wrong way. Somebody will stand at an angle that
should put them on the floor and stay there comfortably. A broom will balance on
its bristles.

There are dozens of these. Mystery Spot, Mystery Hole, Vortex, Gravity House,
Cosmos, Oregon Vortex, Mystery Shack. The signage varies. The building does not.

## The room

It is a box built deliberately out of true, usually around twenty to
twenty-five degrees off level, with no window to the outside and every interior
line — walls, door frames, shelving, the plank the ball rolls on — aligned to
the tilted floor rather than to the ground.

Your sense of upright comes from two places. Your inner ear, which knows where
gravity is. And your eyes, which check that against the world around you.

Under normal conditions they agree, so the question never comes up. Put someone
in a room where every visual reference is tilted and the horizon is hidden, and
the two systems disagree — and vision wins by a wide margin. The room reads as
level. Everything genuinely level then appears wrong, and everything wrong
appears normal.

The ball on the plank really is rolling downhill. You are the thing that is
tilted.

## Why people feel ill

That disagreement between eye and inner ear is the same mismatch that produces
seasickness. Some visitors feel genuinely queasy after a few minutes inside,
which the guides tend to present as the effect of the vortex.

It is not a vortex. It is the mismatch. But the nausea is completely real, which
makes it excellent evidence for whatever the operator wants to claim.

## Where they came from

More or less all of them descend from one attraction: a site near Gold Hill,
Oregon, opened to the public in 1930 as the Oregon Vortex. It was a commercial
success, the design was straightforward to copy, and by the 1940s tilted houses
were opening along tourist routes across the country.

They are, in the most literal sense, the same room built repeatedly. That is not
a criticism. A magic trick does not stop working because you know somebody
invented it.

## What they get right

The good ones are honest in a particular way: they do not really argue with you.
They hand you a ball, tell you to watch, and let the room do the work.

The best of them are also period pieces now. Hand-painted signs, a guide who
has run the same fifteen-minute patter for twenty years, a car embedded in the
front of the building because somebody thought it would pull people off the
road. They are surviving examples of a kind of American roadside commerce that
mostly went away when the interstates bypassed the old routes.

## Doing it properly

Go for the building and the patter as much as the illusion. Fifteen or twenty
minutes is a typical tour and most are seasonal, so check before you drive.

Take somebody who will argue about it. And if you start feeling seasick, step
outside — that is your inner ear filing a complaint, and it stops the moment you
can see the horizon again.
