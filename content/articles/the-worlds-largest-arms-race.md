Frazeysburg, Ohio has the world's largest apple basket. Dresden, Ohio, eight
miles down the road, has the world's largest basket.

Cawker City, Kansas has the world's largest ball of twine. Darwin, Minnesota has
the world's largest ball of twine **made by one man** — a qualifier doing a
great deal of work.

Bemidji, Akeley, Hackensack and Brainerd all have a Paul Bunyan, and they are
all on roughly the same road.

This is not a series of coincidences.

## What these things are actually for

They are advertising, and they have been since the beginning.

A town on a highway has one commercial problem: cars go past it. Everything
else — the shops, the diner, the motel, the tax base — depends on some
proportion of those cars stopping. A roadside giant is the cheapest available
solution, because it converts a fact about your town into a reason to brake.

Dresden was Longaberger country; the company's headquarters was a seven-storey
building shaped like a market basket. Frazeysburg is close enough to share the
identity and evidently close enough to want its own claim. The category had to
be split — *apple* basket — because the plain one was taken.

That splitting is the tell. When two towns hold adjacent records with a
qualifier separating them, you are looking at competition, however politely
nobody says so.

## The record is the point

Guinness rules require the object to perform its function. A record wind chime
has to chime; a record mailbox has to take post. That is why so many of these
things are climbable, sittable, usable — it is not whimsy, it is compliance.

It also means the claims are contestable, which is the engine of the whole
thing. Someone builds bigger. You add a qualifier. Somebody else finds an
unclaimed category.

## Casey did it deliberately

Most towns have one object. Casey, Illinois has more than thirty.

Jim Bolin built the first — a forty-nine foot wind chime, in 2011 — for an
explicitly commercial reason: he wanted a tea shop his wife had opened to
survive, and was not sure a town of 2,300 could support it. So he built
something to bring people to the street, and put signs on I-70.

It worked, and he kept going for fifteen years: twelve Guinness records, and
between one and two thousand visitors a week in a town of 2.6 square miles.

He died in an accident in January 2026.

## What to make of it

It is easy to read these as kitsch, and easy to read them as folk art, and
neither is quite right.

They are infrastructure. A small town looked at an interstate carrying its
customers past at seventy miles an hour and built the largest object it could
afford, because that was a rational response to the problem.

The fact that some of them are beautiful is a bonus nobody was obliged to
provide.
