Somewhere within a few hours of you there is a stretch of road where you can
stop the car, put it in neutral, take your foot off everything, and roll
backwards up the hill.

There are dozens. Spook Hill in Lake Wales, Florida. Judgement Street in
Shullsburg, Wisconsin. Gravity Hill Lane in Talladega County. Spook Rock Road
in Rockland County, New York. Most states have at least one, and several have
three or four.

They all work. They all work for the same reason.

## The reason

The hill is going down. You cannot tell, because you cannot see the horizon.

Judging a slope turns out to be something your eyes do badly on their own. What
they actually do is compare the road against everything around it — the tree
line, the fence posts, the ridge behind, the way a distant field sits against
the sky. Take away a true horizon, tilt the surroundings consistently in one
direction, and the comparison produces the wrong answer with total confidence.

Every gravity hill has the same ingredients. An obstructed skyline, usually
trees or rising ground on both sides. A modest genuine gradient, one to three
degrees. And surroundings sloped harder than the road, so the road *reads* as
uphill against them.

Surveyors have taken instruments to a number of these sites. The road goes
down, every time.

## Why knowing that doesn't spoil it

Because the illusion does not care that you know.

This is the unusual thing about gravity hills, and the reason they are worth
driving to. Most debunked mysteries collapse the moment you learn the answer —
you cannot un-see a wooden foot once you know about it. Here you can hold the
complete explanation in your head, pour a bottle of water on the tarmac, and
still watch it run the wrong way. Your visual system is not persuaded by
argument.

That is a better experience than a mystery. A mystery you either believe or you
don't. This you can test, understand, and still be fooled by, on repeat, for as
long as you feel like standing in the road.

## The stories that got attached

Almost every one of them has a legend, and the legends are close to
interchangeable: a school bus that crashed here, and the handprints of children
pushing your car to safety. A ghost pushing wagons. Something buried nearby.

The school bus story in particular turns up at sites hundreds of miles apart
with no bus, no crash, and no record of either. It is a folklore motif that
travels — the same story arriving in a new place and finding a hill to attach
itself to.

Some sites lean into it. Others are simply a bend in a country road with a
faded mark on the tarmac and a lot of tyre rubber where people have stopped.

## Doing it properly

Go in daylight the first time, so you can see what is coming. These are public
roads, generally narrow, and other people are using them for driving rather
than for physics.

Take water in a bottle to pour out — it is more convincing than the car,
because water obviously cannot be in gear. Find the marked spot; at most sites
somebody has painted one, and the effect is much weaker fifty feet either side.

And take somebody who has not been told the explanation. Watching them work it
out is most of the entertainment.
