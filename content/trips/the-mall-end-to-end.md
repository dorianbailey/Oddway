Everyone walks the Mall. Almost nobody notices what is on it.

This is the same two miles a hundred thousand people cover every week, with the
difference that it stops at the things they walk past. A dog worked into a
memorial wall. A cartoon carved by soldiers, hidden twice. A change in the
colour of the Washington Monument that records twenty-three years of nothing
happening.

Go west to east. The light is better behind you and the Capitol is a
satisfying place to finish.

## Behind the Lincoln Memorial

The **Watergate Steps** were built as a ceremonial landing from the Potomac —
the literal water gate, decades before an office block borrowed the name and
made it mean something else. Concerts were held on the river here.

The **undercroft** beneath the Lincoln Memorial opened to the public in 2026.
It is an enormous concrete cavern of piers holding the monument off the
reclaimed marsh, and it looks nothing like the building above it.

Round the front, **Einstein** sits with his papers on a plaza inlaid with a
star map, one of the few statues in the city that people are actively
encouraged to climb on.

## Two things you have to look for

The **Korean War Veterans Memorial** mural wall was etched from photographs of
the war, and a German shepherd is in there among the faces. It takes a minute.

At the **WWII Memorial** there are two Kilroys — the long-nosed cartoon GIs
scratched onto everything in the war. They were put there deliberately, and
placed where you would have to already know to look.

## The obelisk, three times

Three stops at the Washington Monument, within forty metres of each other, and
all three are the sort of thing you only see once somebody says it.

The **marble stripe** is the obvious one once pointed out: the colour changes
about a third of the way up, because construction stopped in 1854 and did not
resume until 1877, by which time the original quarry could not supply matching
stone.

The **lightning rod** is the reason there is an aluminium cap up there at all —
aluminium being a precious metal in 1884 — and the monument has been struck
often enough to need a proper protection system.

The **mini monument** is a survey marker beside the real one, a small stone
obelisk that looks exactly like the big one seen from very far away.

## Into the Smithsonians

**Bare-Chested George Washington** is Horatio Greenough's 1840 statue of the
first president, bare to the waist in a toga. It was commissioned for the
Capitol rotunda, appalled everyone, and was moved repeatedly before ending up
at American History.

**Martha** was the last passenger pigeon on earth when she died at Cincinnati
Zoo in 1914, and **Sergeant Stubby** was a stray who ended the First World War
as the most decorated dog in the US Army. Both are Smithsonian specimens and
both rotate in and out of display — check before building a day around either.

The **carousel** returned to the Mall in 2026. It carries a civil rights
history: the machine that preceded it operated in a region where amusement
facilities were segregated, and its desegregation was fought for.

The **Enterprise** at Air and Space is the original eleven-foot filming model,
restored, and closer to a religious object than a prop for a good number of
visitors.

## The last stretch

Pei's **Knife Edge** on the East Building is a corner of marble sharpened to
19.5 degrees, polished dark at hand height by decades of people touching it to
check it is real.

Olmsted's **Summerhouse** on the Capitol grounds is a red-brick grotto with a
fountain and a spring, built for people to sit in the shade, and usually empty.

The **Portrait Monument** to Mott, Stanton and Anthony has an unfinished block
of marble behind the three figures. The common story is that it was left for a
fourth woman — a future president — though the sculptor's own explanation was
less tidy.

## The last two need permission

The **Capitol Tile Room** and the **District of Columbia Center Point** are
both inside the Capitol and neither is on the ordinary tour. The tile room
holds spare Minton floor tiles from the 19th-century renovations; the center
point is the marble compass in the Crypt marking where the city's four
quadrants meet.

Arrange these through your representative's office in advance, or finish at the
Summerhouse and treat them as a reason to come back.

## Practical

About three and a half miles, flat the whole way, with eighty minutes of actual
walking. The Smithsonians are free and the museum time is what makes this a
day rather than an afternoon.

Summer on the Mall is genuinely punishing — there is very little shade between
the Washington Monument and the Capitol. The whole route is served by the Mall
side of the Blue, Orange and Silver lines if you want to cut it short.
