Mark Twain, writing about the Mississippi, reckoned there were fifty cliffs
along that river alone claiming to be a Lover's Leap.

He was not exaggerating for effect. There are seven in this index, in five
states, and this index covers a fraction of them.

## The story is the same every time

Two young people from groups that are at war. A forbidden attachment,
discovered. A cliff. A jump, taken together rather than face separation.

Sometimes it is one person rather than two — a woman leaping after a lover who
did not return from a hunt, or rather than marry a man chosen for her. The
details move around; the shape does not.

Hawks Nest in West Virginia has it. Breaks in Virginia has it. Patrick County
has it with a name attached, Morning Flower. Waco has it as Wah-Wah-Tee. El
Dorado County has it as Osaho. Chattanooga has it as Sautee and Nacoochee.

## Where it actually comes from

Not from the people it is attributed to.

The scholarly position, which Wikipedia states plainly, is that these are
**post-contact** tales — they appear in the place-name lore of North America
after European arrival, not before it. They are apocryphal.

The form is much older than America. Cliff-leaping for love runs back through
European literature to Sappho and the Leucadian rock. Settlers arrived carrying
the story, found a dramatic cliff, and attached it — then, in the fashion of the
nineteenth century, dressed it in what they imagined were Native American
clothes.

You can sometimes watch it happen. At El Dorado County the cliff is called
Lover's Leap in a newspaper in **1857**; the full legend of the "Indian maiden
Osaho" appears in the same paper in **1900**, forty-three years later. At Waco
the version everyone knows was popularised by a named author, Decca Lamar West,
in a booklet published in **1912**.

The name comes first. The legend is written afterwards to explain it.

## Why this matters more than pedantry

Because the attribution is not harmless.

These stories put a particular idea into circulation: that Native people were
romantic, doomed, and conveniently in the past tense. That is a nineteenth
century literary fashion, and it was doing work at the time — a nation
displacing living people found it comfortable to imagine them as tragic figures
who had already vanished off a cliff.

The nations these tales are hung on had, and have, their own literatures.
Treating a settler invention as their story does them no favours.

## Go anyway

The cliffs are real and often magnificent, and none of the above is a reason not
to stand on one.

But the interesting thing at a Lover's Leap is not the couple who did not jump.
It is that you are standing at one of at least fifty identical scenes, each
convinced it is the original, and that you can date most of them to a newspaper
office rather than a tradition.
