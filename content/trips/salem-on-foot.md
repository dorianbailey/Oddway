In 1692 nineteen people were hanged at Salem, one was pressed to death under
stones, and several more died in jail. The town has spent three centuries
working out what to do about that, and the answer — visible on any walk down
Essex Street — is everything at once.

There is a bronze Samantha Stephens on a crescent moon four hundred metres from
twenty stone benches carved with the names of the dead. Both are real responses
and both are here. This walk does not try to resolve that, because Salem
hasn't.

Two miles, flat, and dense enough that you will pass most of it whether you
mean to or not.

## Washington Square

The **Salem Witch Museum** has been doing the same thing since 1972: a darkened
room, thirteen life-size stage sets, a narration. It is unfashionable and it is
the introduction most people get.

Round the corner the **Witch Board Museum** is about Ouija boards and
Spiritualism rather than 1692 at all — nineteenth-century talking boards, which
is a different American strangeness entirely.

Then a cluster: the **International Monster Museum**, which you walk through
carrying a lantern; **Witch Mansion**; the **Witch History Museum**; and
**Count Orlok's Nightmare Gallery**, which is film monsters made by people who
make film monsters professionally.

Inside the Peabody Essex is **Yin Yu Tang**, a late-Qing merchant's house taken
apart in Anhui province, shipped to Massachusetts and rebuilt. It has nothing
to do with witches and is one of the most remarkable things in the state.

## Essex Street

The **Bewitched statue** went up in 2005 and people objected, loudly, on the
grounds that a sitcom tribute sits oddly in a town where people were executed.
It is still there and it is always being photographed.

The **Joshua Ward House** is an eighteenth-century mansion built on land
associated with George Corwin — the sheriff who carried out the arrests and the
seizures in 1692. It is a hotel now. **Lyceum Hall**, a few doors down, is
where Alexander Graham Bell made a successful long-distance telephone call in
1877, which no one comes to Salem for.

The **Witch Dungeon Museum** stages a trial reenactment from the surviving
transcripts and then takes you into a recreated jail.

## The two that matter

**The Witch House** was Jonathan Corwin's home. He was one of the magistrates.
It is the only building still standing in Salem with a direct, documented
connection to the trials — everything else associated with 1692 has burned,
been demolished, or been moved.

And then the **Witch Trials Memorial**, off Charter Street beside the old
burying ground. Twenty granite benches, one for each person killed, each carved
with a name and a date and a fragment of what they said in court. The
protestations of innocence are cut into the threshold stones at the entrance,
deliberately placed to be walked over.

It is quiet, it is free, and it is the only thing in town that treats 1692 as
something that happened to people rather than something that happened to Salem.

## Down to the water

**Real Pirates** holds artifacts from the Whydah Gally, the only authenticated
pirate wreck ever recovered. The **Schooner Fame** is a working replica of a
War of 1812 privateer and sails from the wharf.

The **Daniels House** is a First Period house from the 1660s, still an inn,
with the uneven floors and enormous fireplaces that implies.

**Ye Olde Pepper Companie** claims to be the oldest candy company in America
and sells Gibralters, a hard lemon sweet that has been made to the same recipe
since the 1830s.

**The House of the Seven Gables** is the 1668 mansion Hawthorne used for the
novel, with a secret staircase added later by an owner who knew what visitors
wanted.

## Finish in somebody's yard

**Herb Mackey's** is not a museum, an attraction, or anything with hours. It is
a residential yard near the ferry, filled with figures welded from scrap metal
and machine parts by the man who lives there. Look from the pavement.

## Practical

Two miles, flat, about forty-five minutes of walking. Doing all twenty of these
properly is two days; most people pick six.

October is the point and October is unbearable — the town takes several hundred
thousand visitors in a month of about forty thousand residents, and the
museums sell timed tickets that go weeks ahead. February gets you the same
Witch House with nobody in it.

The memorial, the Bewitched statue and Mackey's yard are free and always open.
Everything else has hours, and the seasonal attractions close for the winter.
