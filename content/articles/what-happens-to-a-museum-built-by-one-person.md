Almost every museum in this index was built by one person who could not let
something go.

That is what separates them from institutions. A county historical society
survives its founders because it was designed to. A single-subject museum on a
state highway is one obsessive with a lease, and it lasts exactly as long as
they do.

The Bigfoot Discovery Museum in Felton, California is the clearest case, because
it has now finished.

## The setup

Michael Rugg was four years old, camping with his family in Humboldt County,
when he saw something in the trees and — as he told it for the rest of his life
— looked it in the eye. His parents told him he had imagined it.

He went to Stanford on a full scholarship, graduated in 1968, and spent a long
career in Silicon Valley. When the dot-com bubble burst he took the money and
opened a museum on Highway 9, with his wife Paula Yarr, in 2004.

The San Lorenzo Valley is not a Bigfoot hotspot. The museum was there because
Rugg was there.

## What was in it

A single-storey building among the redwoods, packed tight. Plaster casts of
tracks. A map with pins for around a hundred and fifty local sightings. Bigfoot
merchandise from the 1970s boom, when the creature was briefly everywhere. A
jacket worn by the crew of *Harry and the Hendersons*. The Patterson-Gimlin film
running on a loop.

Admission was free. Donations were suggested at two to five dollars. Between
thirty and seventy people came through on an average day, and the thing every
single visitor account mentions is Rugg himself — that if you were lucky he
would come out and talk to you for an hour, and that his conviction was
infectious whether or not you shared it.

Asked how he separated real reports from hoaxes, he said he could tell by
looking someone in the eye.

## How it ended

Not dramatically. The way these usually do.

Visitors raised more than $16,000 during the pandemic to keep it open. Then
Rugg's health went — chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, an oxygen tank he
had to carry, weekly hunting trips abandoned. Six days a week became four. He
listed the property in October 2024 and closed for good in 2025, after twenty
years.

*It's a real bummer*, he said about it, which is about as much self-pity as
appears anywhere in the record.

## The part that matters

The collection did not scatter.

Most of it went north, to Cliff Barackman's North American Bigfoot Center in
Boring, Oregon — which is itself a single-subject museum built by one person who
could not let it go. One obsessive's life work handed to another obsessive who
would understand what it was.

That is the good outcome, and it is not the usual one. Far more often the
building sells, the collection goes to auction in lots, and forty years of one
person's attention is distributed among strangers who wanted a plaster cast for
a rec room.

## Why to go while they are open

There are museums in this index run by people in their seventies and eighties,
in buildings they own, funded by a donation jar.

They are not permanent. There is no succession plan, because there is rarely
anything to succeed to except one person's knowledge of their own collection —
which is the actual exhibit, and which does not transfer.

If a small museum on a back highway is on your route and open, stop. The
building will probably still be there in ten years. The person behind the
counter who can tell you what everything is may not be.
