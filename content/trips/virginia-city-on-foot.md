Virginia City sits on a mountainside, which means this walk has a correct
direction: start at the top and let the hill do the work.

The Comstock Lode made this the richest place in Nevada and for a while one of
the richest in the country. Twenty-five thousand people lived here in the
1870s. Around eight hundred do now, and what is left is an unusually dense run
of buildings that nobody ever had a reason to knock down.

## Start with the dead

The **Silver Terrace Cemeteries** are not one cemetery but a dozen, climbing
the hill in separate plots: Masonic, Catholic, Odd Fellows, Firemen's, the
Pacific Coast Pioneers. Mining towns sorted themselves by fraternal order and
by nation, and the hillside is the clearest surviving map of who the Comstock
actually was.

Walk down from here rather than up. It is a mile to the next stop and all of it
downhill.

## Down C Street

The **Fourth Ward School** is a four-storey wooden building from 1876, Second
Empire, with its classrooms intact — the kind of public architecture a town
builds when it believes the money is permanent.

Then the run along C Street: **The Way It Was Museum**, packed with mining
machinery and photographs in the old manner; **St. Mary in the Mountains**,
Nevada's oldest working Catholic church, Gothic Revival, rebuilt after the fire
of 1875 took most of the town; and the **Mackay Mansion**, which was John
Mackay's house when he was one of the four men who controlled the Lode.

## The saloon with a mine in it

The **Ponderosa** is a functioning bar. Behind it is the entrance to a real
tunnel, and the tour goes into the hillside from the back of the room. It is
the most concise illustration available of what this town was built on and
what it was built for.

Twenty metres on is the **Washoe Club**, a preserved saloon and museum with a
long reputation for ghost stories, and past that the **Silver Queen**, whose
portrait is made of several thousand silver dollars.

**Piper's Opera House** hosted touring performers, prize fights and political
meetings; there have been three Pipers, fire having taken the first two.

## Finish underground

**Chollar Mine** is the last stop and a proper one: a guided descent into
preserved workings, square-set timbering and all. It is about two thirds of a
mile south of the opera house, which is the second-longest leg of the day and
the only one you might regret at altitude.

## Practical

Three miles end to end, most of it gently downhill if you go in this order, at
six thousand feet.

The cemeteries and the churchyard are free and always open. Chollar and the
Ponderosa are ticketed and run on scheduled tours, so check times before you
build the day around them. The Mackay Mansion and Piper's both keep limited
hours that vary by season.

Doing this in reverse means finishing with a mile of uphill to the cemeteries.
That is a real climb, not a figure of speech.
